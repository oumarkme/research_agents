# Humanize prose, never hide AI use

The plugin should humanize text by editing it: remove measured stylistic tells, add the author's specifics, clean formatting residue, and prove with a script that no number, identifier, or citation changed. It should not try to defeat watermarks or detectors, and it should say so. Four things get called an "AI watermark", and they behave differently: a statistical watermark in word choice (SynthID-Text, and Anthropic's reported Claude scheme), invisible characters, stylistic habits, and classifier scores. Only the last three are within an editor's reach. The statistical watermark is a property of how words were sampled, and the providers themselves say that light edits probably leave it and that a full rewrite removes or sharply weakens it. That is a reason to disclose AI use, not a goal to engineer toward. The best-supported markers are specific words (the "delves" family), present participial clauses, nominalizations, and em-dash habits, and these drift with each model generation, so any fixed word list decays. Paraphrasing tools that optimize against a detector are the strongest evasion method on record, but they trade away meaning and quality, and no study measured how often they alter numbers or citations. I recommend one standalone `humanizer` skill that takes over `references/humanizer.md` from the `writing` skill, with a preservation-diff script, an invisible-Unicode check, a lint upgrade, and six evals, and no new agent. Sources below are flagged where they rest on snippets, vendors, preprints, or secondary summaries.

## A watermark is a sampling key, and editing prose does not target it

Statistical text watermarks change how the next word is drawn so that a key holder can test for the bias later. Kirchenbauer and colleagues promote a keyed "green" subset of tokens and detect it with a p-value ([arXiv 2301.10226](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.10226)). SynthID-Text uses tournament sampling and needs no retraining. It was tested on nearly 20 million Gemini responses with no measurable quality loss in user feedback ([PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11499265/); the Nature page itself was not read, so this is snippet-level). Google's documentation says the mark tolerates cropping, a few word changes, and mild paraphrasing, is weaker on factual answers, "can drop substantially" under heavy rewriting or translation, and is "not designed to stop determined adversaries" ([Google](https://ai.google.dev/responsible/docs/safeguards/synthid)).

Anthropic published a page on 14 August 2026 (updated 1 September). It states that Claude models launched on or after 2 August 2026 carry "a version of the SynthID-Text approach". Only the randomness used to choose among candidate words changes, and "nothing is added to the text and there are no hidden characters" ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-text-watermark)). The same page says light editing probably will not remove the mark and a complete rewrite will. The mark cannot separate "Claude wrote this" from "Claude heavily edited this", and it is weak on short and factual text. Code is generally not marked but code comments can be, and Claude translations are marked. The detection API is in private preview for regulators, researchers, and comparable bodies. **Flag:** only one researcher read this page, and no second source confirms its details. IEEE Spectrum reports the announcement as 11 August rather than 14 August and notes that Anthropic declined to say how its method differs from SynthID-Text ([IEEE Spectrum](https://spectrum.ieee.org/ai-watermark-text-anthropic-openai)). The same article says OpenAI has not shipped a text watermark; the only OpenAI evidence is a 2024 secondary news report ([Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/openai-confirms-its-looking-into-text-watermarking-for-chatgpt-that-could-expose-cheating-students-223920531.html)), so OpenAI's current status is unverified. **Flag:** both Anthropic and IEEE tie the transparency requirement to the EU AI Act applying from 2 August 2026, but nobody checked the Act text or any later delay, so treat that date as unconfirmed.

The academic literature agrees that these marks are weak by design. Recursive paraphrasing lowers detection for watermark, neural, and retrieval detectors, and watermarked text can be spoofed so that human writing is flagged ([Sadasivan et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11156)). A theory result says strong watermarking is impossible under natural assumptions ([Zhang et al., snippet-only](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v235/zhang24o.html)). I did not find a reputable general threshold for how much rewriting defeats a given scheme, and I do not give one: any such number is scheme-specific, and the plugin has no legitimate use for it. Two consequences matter for design. A detector result is a probability for one provider's key, so a negative says little and a positive is not proof of who wrote the text. And whatever an honest editing pass does to a watermark is a side effect of changing the words, not a feature the skill can promise or measure. The model writing a document in the plugin may itself be a post-August-2026 Claude. The safe assumption is that raw drafts can carry the mark until the author rewrites them in their own words. Disclosure, not editing, is what protects the author.

Invisible characters are a separate, smaller issue. Anthropic says its scheme adds none. Reports of U+202F, U+200B, and curly quotes in ChatGPT-family output come from a detector vendor's blog and Reddit threads, and the vendor itself says they are not a watermark ([Originality.ai](https://originality.ai/pt/blog/invisible-text-detector-remover); vendor, anecdotal). The C2PA 2.3 specification lists plain text as a supported type, and the third-party `c2patxt` package encodes a mark as Unicode variation selectors appended to text ([PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/c2patxt/); third-party, snippet-level). No evidence shows that a major chat product does this today. Checking for and removing these characters is formatting hygiene that LaTeX, diff, and journal systems need anyway. It does not touch a statistical watermark.

Stylistic fingerprints and classifier scores sit on the other side of the line. They are properties of the writing, so honest editing changes them. Classifiers are unreliable as evidence. GPT detectors misclassified non-native English writing as AI-generated, and simple prompts that raise word variety both reduced the bias and evaded the detectors ([Liang et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.02819)). OpenAI withdrew its own classifier in July 2023 for low accuracy ([Search Engine Land](https://searchengineland.com/openai-ai-classifier-no-longer-available-429912); secondary). A University of Chicago working paper found Pangram the only one of four commercial and open tools to hold a 0.5% false-positive cap ([BFI WP 2025-116](https://bfi.uchicago.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/BFI_WP_2025-116.pdf); not peer reviewed). Pangram's own claims of resistance to humanizers are vendor statements. A model-attribution classifier reached 97.1% accuracy in naming which of five chatbots wrote a text, and its signal survived rewriting and translation ([Sun et al., ICML 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.12150); snippet-only). Frequent LLM users, voting as a panel of five, misclassified 1 of 300 articles, beating most detectors, including on humanized text ([Russell et al., ACL 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.15654); abstract-level). The residual cues those readers use are vocabulary plus formality, originality, and clarity. A skill cannot add originality, which is why the author's own specifics must be the input.

## Stylistic tells are measurable, and the list changes with every model generation

The strongest evidence is population-level. Kobak and colleagues compared 15.1 million PubMed abstracts and found 454 excess words in 2024, against a pandemic-era peak of 190 in 2021; 66% of the 2024 style words were verbs and 14% adjectives. The ratios were highest for "delves" (28.0), "underscores" (13.8), and "showcasing" (10.7), and the largest absolute gaps were for "potential", "findings", and "crucial". The lower-bound share of LLM-processed 2024 abstracts was 13.5%, ranging from under 5% to over 40% across subcorpora ([Kobak et al., arXiv 2406.07016](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.07016v5); Science Advances 2025, read through the preprint). A multi-database analysis found the marker words co-occurring far more often in 2024 than in 2022 (underscore with pivotal, r = 0.449 against about 0.03), which supports treating a cluster of markers as stronger evidence than one word ([arXiv 2509.09596](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.09596); abstract-level). A word alone proves nothing, and people are adopting these words too ([Geng and Trotta](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.08627v2); snippet-only).

Syntax carries more signal than vocabulary. Reinhart and colleagues (PNAS 2025) compared instruction-tuned models with 8,290 human texts. GPT-4o used present participial clauses at 5.3 times the human rate and nominalizations at 2.1 times, while agentless passives ran at about half the human rate. Base models sat closest to human rates, and larger size did not close the gap ([arXiv 2410.16107](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.16107)). Two design consequences follow. The "-ing" tail and noun-heavy phrasing are the checks to automate, and the passive voice in a Methods section is not an AI tell, so the lint must not push every passive into the active voice. A 2026 preprint on preprint corpora reports flatter, more uniform syntax after late 2022, with maximum dependency depth and its variation falling ([arXiv 2610.26008](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.26008); preprint, abstract-level). Em-dash rates are model-specific and contested, ranging from 0.0 to 9.1 per 1,000 words across 12 models in one independent preprint ([arXiv 2603.27006](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.27006)); blog reports conflict on which model uses most. The existing no-em-dash rule is therefore a house style with weak evidence behind it, which is acceptable if the skill says so.

Structural folk patterns have the thinnest support. The Wikipedia field guide "Signs of AI writing" lists negative parallelism, rule-of-three lists, vague attribution, and inflated significance, and itself says these are observations and not proof ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing); I read only the first 100,000 of about 243,000 characters). I found no peer-reviewed frequency data for negative parallelism, rule of three, or paragraph uniformity. They should stay in the checklist, ranked below the measured items and labeled heuristic.

Drift is the main reason a fixed list fails. "Delve" fell sharply once it was publicized, according to one search-snippet source, yet PMC full text through July 2025 shows "underscore" and "intricate" still rising ([arXiv 2509.09596](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.09596)). A Korean study of 398,296 abstracts finds the marker set turning over with successive model generations, with English abstracts of the same articles leading by about a year ([arXiv 2609.07447](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.07447); working paper). Wikipedia's era-by-era word lists (GPT-4, GPT-4o, GPT-5, then "leverage" and "multifaceted" with shorter sentences from mid-2026) are community observation, not measurement. I found no peer-reviewed study separating Claude 4.x or 5, GPT-5, or Gemini 3 vocabulary in scientific text. Avoiding the listed words also leaves its own gap in the word distribution, so avoidance alone does not make text human. For Taiwan Traditional Chinese the evidence is thinnest. Benchmarks show models favoring Simplified-Chinese vocabulary in regional term tasks ([SC-TC-Bench, arXiv 2505.22645](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.22645); snippet-only), and a practitioner talk describes zh-CN term leakage and unstable punctuation, but no peer-reviewed list of zh-TW tells exists. The existing OpenCC check in `lint_prose.py` is the right tool there.

## Only meaning-preserving editing has a place in a lab plugin

Detector-guided paraphrasing is the best-documented way to lower a detector score. It cut true-positive rate at 1% false positives by 87.88% on average across detector families, with authors reporting "mostly slight" quality loss ([Cheng et al., NeurIPS 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.07001); abstract-level). DIPPER dropped DetectGPT from 70.3% to 4.6% at the same operating point ([Krishna et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.13408)). Plain LLM paraphrase, by contrast, made two detectors catch more (+8.57% and +15.03%), so rewriting alone is not a dependable route either. These numbers measure detector scores, not reader value, and the quality judgments come from the attack authors. The plugin should not copy them.

Prompt-based approaches have weaker evidence. Few-shot prompts with author samples raised style-matching accuracy up to 23.5 times over zero-shot, yet outputs stayed less unpredictable than human essays ([arXiv 2509.24930](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.24930v1); preprint, snippet). A blog write-up reports detectors missing 10% to 18% of few-shot imitations ([ai2.work](https://ai2.work/blog/ai-detectors-miss-18-of-style-mimicked-text-new-study-finds); secondary, small samples). Commercial humanizers show only vendor or affiliate-blog bypass rates, and the one structured study of 19 tools was written by a detector vendor ([arXiv 2501.03437](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.03437); abstract-level). I found no independent evaluation of any humanizer skill, and no study that measured fine-tuning on human text or human post-editing for both detector score and quality. I also found no direct measurement of whether banning one word raises the frequency of another. The only quality-oriented evidence is on readers: in a CHI 2026 preprint, ML-expert readers could not tell human, LLM-edited, and LLM-generated abstracts apart, yet rated LLM-edited best and fully generated lowest ([Akpinar et al., arXiv 2601.15556](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.15556); preprint, snippet). That favors an author-content-first workflow, where the model edits human text.

The damage risks are concrete. Synonym-swapping tools produce "tortured phrases" such as "fake neural organization" for "artificial neural network"; the Problematic Paper Screener built on such fingerprints has supported over 1,000 retractions ([Peer Review Congress abstract](https://peerreviewcongress.org/abstract/screening-articles-for-tortured-phrases-with-a-regular-expressions-based-detector); secondary figures). Higher lexical diversity in paraphrase makes meaning harder to keep ([DIPPER PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2303.13408); snippet). LLM rewriting can transpose authors, garble titles, and produce invalid DOIs, and 2% to 6% of 2025 proceedings papers in one report contained unverifiable citations ([arXiv 2602.05930](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.05930.pdf); snippet-only, and not about humanizers). Nobody has measured how often humanizer tools change numbers, units, gene symbols, or HGVS strings, so the safeguard must be mechanical and not assumed: mask protected spans before the model sees the text, then diff every protected token afterward. Surface edits can also hide fabricated or unsupported content, so the skill must never be the last check on a claim.

## Policies separate polishing from concealment

Every body surveyed bars AI authorship and holds the human authors responsible. ICMJE tells journals to require disclosure at submission in both the cover letter and the work, and says nondisclosure "may be construed as misconduct in some circumstances" ([ICMJE](https://www.icmje.org/recommendations/browse/artificial-intelligence/ai-use-by-authors.html)). COPE requires disclosure in Materials and Methods naming the tool and how it was used ([COPE](https://publicationethics.org/guidance/cope-position/authorship-and-ai-tools)). Elsevier (policy updated July 2026) exempts "basic checks of grammar, spelling and punctuation" and otherwise wants a declaration above the references ([Elsevier](https://www.elsevier.com/about/policies-and-standards/the-use-of-generative-ai-and-ai-assisted-technologies-in-writing-for-elsevier)). Wiley exempts AI "used solely for spelling, grammar, and general editing" ([Wiley](https://authors.wiley.com/ethics-guidelines/index.html)), and PLOS does not require disclosure for language improvements ([PLOS](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/s/ethical-publishing-practice)). Springer Nature's page says copy-editing need not be declared but also says use "should be declared", so the exemption is uncertain ([Springer Nature](https://group.springernature.com/gp/group/ai/ai-guidance-for-our-researchers-and-communities)). Bioinformatics accepts LLMs as a spelling and grammar aid and for translation, wants disclosure in the cover letter and Methods or Acknowledgements, and says drafting a paper from a prompt is not acceptable ([Bioinformatics](https://academic.oup.com/bioinformatics/pages/general_instructions)). Frontiers requires acknowledgement of AI-edited content with tool name and version ([Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/guidelines/policies-and-publication-ethics)). Nature Portfolio, Science, MDPI, and Cell Press primary pages were not read (cookie gate or 403), so their rules rest on secondary summaries, one of which disagreed with the others; re-check them before submission.

The wording of the exemptions covers fixing errors. Whether an LLM that restructures sentences for style falls inside "language editing" is my reading, not any publisher's statement, and the cautious default is to declare any pass in which a model rewrote sentences. Grant and institutional rules are firmer. NIH notice NOT-OD-25-132 says applications "substantially developed by AI" will not be considered, allows post-award misconduct referral, and states NIH uses AI-detection technology ([NIH](https://grants.nih.gov/grants/guide/notice-files/NOT-OD-25-132.html)). The NTU June 2026 guideline does not ban generative AI as a research aid. It requires disclosure of tool name, version, date, purpose, operation, and degree of involvement, and asks authors to keep logs of key prompts, outputs, and human revisions. It warns that heavy, influential, undisclosed use can amount to plagiarism, including AI rewriting of one's own published work ([NTU ORI PDF](https://ori.ntu.edu.tw/ethics/download/fileSn/25)). I found no NSTC research-writing guideline and no NTUH-specific policy, so the NTU text is the likely applicable one for an NTUH postdoc, pending confirmation with the research integrity office. Enforcement so far is reactive: a reader found "Regenerate response" in a Physica Scripta paper and it was retracted ([Asiae summary](https://www.asiae.co.kr/en/article/2023091209440262767); secondary). Aggregate vocabulary shifts are measurable, so marker words are a screening risk even without a detector.

The honest framing for the report and for the skill is three separate goals. Cleaning formatting residue is legitimate. Rewriting prose in the author's own voice is legitimate, reduces stylistic tells, and changes any statistical mark only as a side effect. Concealing AI use from a venue that requires disclosure is not endorsed, and this report supplies no recipe for it. A truthful disclosure and natural-reading prose are compatible, and a declared use cannot be "caught".

## One standalone skill, no new agent

I recommend a standalone `humanizer` skill and not a new agent. The CLAUDE.md in this lab already makes the main session the writer and asks for delegation only for isolation, independence, parallelism, or long jobs. A humanizer pass on a paragraph is a few tool calls, so an agent adds a fresh context that has not seen the author's intent, which is where meaning drift enters. The `writer` agent already preloads `writing`; add `humanizer` to its `skills:` list. For a long manuscript, the independence case is real, and the existing `reviewer` agent can do one read-only check of the edited file against the original using the preservation diff, without a new agent file. The `writing` skill should keep its workflow and call `humanizer` at step 3; `references/humanizer.md` moves to the new skill and `writing/references/humanizer.md` becomes a two-line pointer so older instructions still resolve.

The current files show where the upgrades go. `references/humanizer.md` is a good short catalog, but it lists vocabulary first, mixes measured and folk items, and has no era tags. `scripts/lint_prose.py` flags stock words, structure patterns, and em dashes, and it already catches curly quotes and Simplified characters. It has no invisible-Unicode check, no preservation check, and no measurement of the participial and nominalization signals that Reinhart found. The two files also disagree: the humanizer text allows one em dash per page, while the linter flags every one.

The SKILL.md should stay under 500 lines (Anthropic's authoring guidance, [best practices](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/agents-and-tools/agent-skills/best-practices)), with a third-person description under 1,024 characters. Its workflow has five steps. Mask protected spans (numbers with units, P values, gene and variant strings, accessions, DOIs, citation keys, URLs, code, tables, headings). Audit and rank the tells. Make the smallest edit that removes each tell without adding facts. Unmask and run the two scripts. Show a word-level diff to the author. It states three rules at the top: never add facts, names, numbers, or references; edit the author's text and do not generate a draft to be humanized; and never evaluate success by a detector score. The references sit one level deep. `tells.md` ranks measured syntax and vocabulary first and folk structure second, each with its source and a date. `era-words.md` is a dated word list with a refresh recipe from the `berenslab/llm-excess-vocab` repository, which holds the Kobak lists (not fetched here). `preservation.md` defines the protected-span grammar. `disclosure.md` holds a dated table of journal, funder, and NTU rules, a declaration template, and the instruction to re-check the primary page before submission. `limits.md` is short. The declaration template can be adapted from the elements journals ask for: tool, version, date, purpose, scope of changes, and who checked the output. `disclosure.md` also points to the `analysis-log` skill for the prompt and revision log that NTU asks authors to keep.

The scripts are where the evidence turns into checks.

| Script or check | What it does | Why |
|---|---|---|
| `preserve_diff.py original edited` | Extracts protected tokens as multisets from both files (numbers with units, P values, percentages, HGVS c./p./g. strings, rs numbers, accessions such as NM_, ENST, GCA_, SRR, PRJNA, DOIs, PMIDs, URLs, citation keys and author-year citations, code spans, heading text, figure and table references). Exits nonzero on any difference and prints each | No study measures humanizer damage, so the check is mechanical, not assumed |
| `unicode_check.py file` | Reports line, column, code point, name, and category for Cf, nonstandard Zs (NBSP, U+202F), Cc other than newline and tab, variation selectors, the tag block, U+00AD, and U+2011; `--fix` replaces only a named safe set | Hygiene for LaTeX, diff, and journal systems; separate from statistical marks |
| Lint additions | Counts present participial tails per 100 sentences, nominalization-suffix density, sentence-length mean and spread, paragraph-opening repeats, era-tagged word hits; keeps agentless passives unflagged in Methods | Reinhart effect sizes and the flatter-syntax result |
| Lint fixes | Make the em-dash rule consistent with the humanizer text; keep OpenCC for zh-TW | Removes the one-per-page versus zero conflict |

Two cautions about the Unicode script come from my own reasoning, not a source. NFKC normalization over a whole file is unsafe for genomics text. It turns the micro sign into a Greek mu, superscripts such as the 2 in h² into plain digits, and ligatures into letter pairs. Replace only listed characters. And zero-width joiners and the ideographic space are legitimate in some scripts and in zh-TW typesetting, so the script should warn on them and not fix them by default.

Evals should follow Anthropic's advice to write at least three before extensive documentation and to test with Haiku, Sonnet, and Opus. Six cases cover the risks. A paragraph with planted tells and 12 protected tokens should leave the diff empty and cut lint flags. A methods paragraph with HGVS variants, accessions, a table, and a citation list must come back unchanged in every protected span. A paragraph already written by a human should be edited minimally, which tests false positives. A request to make a paragraph "undetectable by Turnitin and SynthID" should produce an ordinary edit, a reminder to disclose, and no attempt to optimize against a detector. A file seeded with U+200B, U+202F, and NBSP should be reported and cleaned without touching a seeded micro sign and superscript. A zh-TW paragraph should keep English technical terms, avoid Simplified characters, and use full-width punctuation. Grade by script results plus a blinded human rating of a sample, never by detector output.

What the skill may claim is narrow and should be written into `limits.md` and the README. It reduces measured stylistic tells and formatting residue, it checks that numbers, identifiers, and citations did not change, and it prompts the author to disclose. It does not guarantee any detector result, does not detect or remove statistical watermarks, cannot replace the author's own specifics, and cannot verify that a claim is true. Raw Claude drafts from post-August-2026 models may carry a provider watermark, and light editing may leave it; the author should disclose AI assistance where the venue requires it instead of relying on edits. The word lists are dated and will decay. Restate five unverified points in the skill: the Anthropic page (single reader), the 2 August 2026 EU date, OpenAI's current watermark status, the primary text of the Nature, Science, MDPI, and Cell Press policies, and any NTUH-specific rule.

## Conclusion

The central finding is that "humanizing" splits into two jobs that this evidence keeps apart. Making prose read as a specialist wrote it is an editing problem with measurable targets (participial tails, nominalizations, the current excess words) and a quality payoff, since readers rated LLM-edited human text best. Defeating a watermark or detector is a different goal with its own literature, no quality guarantee, and a conflict with the disclosure rules that ICMJE, COPE, and NTU spell out. The skill should do the first and refuse to optimize for the second.

The practical gain over the current `writing` pass is verification, not a longer word list. A preservation diff and a Unicode report turn "do not damage the science" into a command that fails, and the six evals give the first measured check on whether the humanizer helps. The notes could not settle whether banned-word suppression only shifts the tells to new words. Test that on the plugin's own outputs, scored against Kobak-style frequency tables, before the word list grows.
