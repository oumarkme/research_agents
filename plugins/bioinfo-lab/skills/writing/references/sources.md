# Sources behind the writing rules

Checked 2026-10-02. "Fetched" = page text retrieved; "snippet" = quoted from a search result because direct fetch failed (403 or paywall redirect) — re-check wording before citing the source in a paper.

* Nature, formatting guide (fetched): write clearly and simply for readers in other disciplines; avoid technical jargon, explain essential specialised terms; keep abbreviations to a minimum and avoid them in titles and abstracts; title ≤ 75 characters without numbers, acronyms, abbreviations, or punctuation; summary paragraph ideally ≤ 200 words without numbers, abbreviations, or measurements unless essential, aimed at readers outside the discipline; one space between number and unit, SI, thousands separated by commas; error bars and statistics defined in legends. https://www.nature.com/nature/for-authors/formatting-guide
* Nature Portfolio editorial policy on priority and novelty claims and self-evaluative language (snippet, via search results from Nature Human Behaviour and Nature Cancer editorials): authors are asked to avoid words denoting primacy (first, new, novel) and to temper claims of extraordinary significance, because priority claims cannot be verified and evaluation is left to readers. https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-021-01068-x ; https://www.nature.com/articles/s43018-023-00685-x
* Nature Portfolio inclusive language and descriptors guidance (snippet): use inclusive, non-stigmatizing language; avoid group descriptors unless relevant. https://www.nature.com/nature-portfolio/editorial-policies/ethics-and-biosecurity
* Science, instructions for authors of new research articles (snippet only; page returned HTTP 403): active voice where suitable; avoid jargon; define abbreviations at first mention. https://www.science.org/content/page/instructions-authors-new-research-articles
* Cell Press, STAR Methods guide (snippet only; PDF returned 403): structured, transparent methods; key resources table; citations are not a substitute for procedure details. https://www.cell.com/pb-assets/journals/research/cell/methods/Methods_Guide_general-1678470557763.pdf
* ICMJE, Recommendations, preparing for submission (fetched): use only standard abbreviations, spell out at first mention, none in titles; clarity in Methods so a reader can judge and verify; quantify findings with uncertainty (e.g., CI) and do not rely on P values alone; distinguish prespecified from exploratory analyses; define statistical terms and software; report sex and gender appropriately; state limitations; do not cite AI-generated material as a primary source. https://www.icmje.org/recommendations/browse/manuscript-preparation/preparing-for-submission.html
* Gopen, G. D., & Swan, J. A. (1990). The science of scientific writing. *American Scientist, 78*(6), 550–558. Reader-expectation principles: subject near verb; old before new; stress position; one point per unit; topic position gives context. (Search results only; PDF not parsed.)
* American Psychological Association. (2020). *Publication manual of the American Psychological Association* (7th ed.). Bias-free language; first person ("we"); APA citation format.
* Wasserstein, R. L., & Lazar, N. A. (2016). The ASA statement on p-values: Context, process, and purpose. *The American Statistician, 70*(2), 129–133. Basis for not relying on P values alone.

## Sources for the humanizer, American English, and Taiwan terminology rules

* Wikipedia, "Signs of AI writing" (WikiProject AI Cleanup; fetched 2026-10-02): vocabulary tells by period (*delve, intricate, pivotal, testament, tapestry*; later *align with, enhance, fostering, showcasing*; most recently *emphasizing, highlighting*), avoidance of *is/are* in favor of *serves as/features/marks*, negative parallelisms ("not just X but Y"), trailing *-ing* phrases that add vague significance, vague attribution ("experts argue"), promotional tone, outline-like conclusions, excessive boldface and em dashes, title case, emoji. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing
* Kobak, D., et al. (2025). Delving into LLM-assisted writing in biomedical publications through excess vocabulary. *Science Advances*. https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.adt3813 (snippet via search results; the full author list, volume, and pages were not retrieved and must be confirmed before citing): analysis of more than 15 million PubMed abstracts finds excess use of style words (verbs and adjectives, e.g., *delves, underscores, showcasing*) after 2023 and estimates that at least 13.5% of 2024 abstracts were LLM-processed.
* APA Style: American English; spelling follows Merriam-Webster's Collegiate Dictionary (first-listed form); periods and commas inside closing quotation marks; colons and semicolons outside (snippet via search results; https://www.scribbr.com/apa-style/apa-language-guidelines/ summarizes the APA rule). Merriam-Webster: https://www.merriam-webster.com/
