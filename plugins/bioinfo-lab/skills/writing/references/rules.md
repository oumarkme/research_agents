# writing: detailed rules

## 1. Simple and direct

* One idea per sentence; average 15–20 words; split any sentence over about 30 words. Plain words: *use*, not *utilize*; *show*, not *demonstrate*; *about*, not *approximately* (unless exactness matters); *to*, not *in order to*.
* Active voice and "we" for what the authors did and found ("We sequenced 48 accessions"). Passive only when the actor is irrelevant or unknown, or the object is the topic ("Samples were stored at −80 °C"). Never "the authors" for yourselves.
* Replace nominalizations with verbs: *we analyzed*, not *we performed an analysis of*; *X increased*, not *there was an increase in X*.
* Cut filler: *it is worth noting that*, *a total of*, *due to the fact that*, *as a matter of fact*, *very*, *really*, *quite*, *basically*, *obviously*, *clearly* (as an intensifier), *interestingly*.
* Keep related words together: subject close to its verb; modifiers next to what they modify (Gopen & Swan, 1990).

## 2. Unambiguous

* One term per concept, used identically throughout (do not vary *accession*, *line*, *genotype*, *sample* for the same thing). Define each term once; use established field terminology; do not coin terms.
* Abbreviations: few. Spell out at first use with the abbreviation in parentheses, in the abstract and again in the main text; standard units and universal abbreviations (DNA, RNA, SD, SEM) need no definition. None in titles; avoid in abstracts. Use an abbreviation only if the term recurs at least three times.
* Pronouns and demonstratives need a clear referent: never start a sentence with a bare *This*, *It*, *They*, *Such*; write *This difference* / *This method*.
* Avoid vague quantity and comparison: replace *a lot*, *several*, *many*, *high*, *improved*, *better*, *similar* with a number, a unit, and a comparator ("higher than the control by 12%, 95% CI 8–16%").
* Avoid *respectively* chains, stacked noun strings (more than three nouns), and long nested parentheses. State direction and comparator in every comparison.
* *Significant* only means statistically significant, with the test and P value or interval; otherwise use *large*, *marked* (with numbers). *Correlation* and *association* are not *effect* or *cause*; use *affects*, *causes*, *leads to*, *drives* only when the design (randomization, intervention, Mendelian randomization, knockout) supports causality. Prefer *associated with*, *was higher in*.
* Units: SI, one space between number and unit (5 kb, 37 °C, 10 mM), thousands with commas (1,000), numerals for measurements, words for one to nine otherwise, no starting a sentence with a numeral. Use ISO-like dates (2026-10-02) in files; define genome assembly and annotation versions.
* Field conventions: gene symbols in italics (*TP53*), protein symbols upright; species names in Latin italics at first mention (*Oryza sativa*); give reference genome build, software name, version, and key parameters.

## 3. Neutral and calibrated

* No priority or self-evaluation: avoid *novel*, *first*, *new* (as a claim), *unprecedented*, *groundbreaking*, *remarkable*, *striking*, *dramatic*, *superior*, *state-of-the-art*, *highly*, *robust* (unless statistical), *crucial*, *key* (as praise), *paradigm*. Nature Portfolio asks authors to remove priority claims and qualitative evaluation of their own work; readers judge value.
* Match strength to evidence: *show* only for direct, replicated evidence; *suggest*, *indicate*, *is consistent with* for indirect evidence; *may* for a hypothesis, stated once, not stacked (*may possibly suggest*).
* Report effects with size and uncertainty (estimate, 95% CI, n), not P values alone; give exact P (P = 0.003; P < 0.001 for tiny values), the test, sidedness, and multiple-testing correction. Separate prespecified from exploratory analyses. Define error bars (SD, SEM, CI) and n in captions (ICMJE; Nature).
* Report negative and null results as plainly as positive ones. State limitations in the Discussion (sample, design, confounding, generalizability).
* Criticize ideas and methods, not people. Cite prior work fairly with APA 7 (Author, Year; & for two authors in parentheses; *et al.* for three or more).
* Inclusive, non-stigmatizing language; mention sex, gender, ancestry, age, or disability only when relevant; use *sex* for biological and *gender* for social variables; people-first or the community's preferred terms; "participants", not "subjects".

## 4. Structure and tense

* Paragraph: a topic sentence stating the point, then support, ending on the new or important information (stress position). One point per paragraph; 3–6 sentences. Start sentences with familiar information and end with new information; link by repeating the key term, not by *Moreover*/*Furthermore* chains (Gopen & Swan, 1990).
* Title (Nature-style): ≤ 75 characters, states the finding or subject, no abbreviations, numbers, or punctuation tricks, understandable outside the field. No question or "Towards/A study of" titles.
* Abstract (≤ 200 words unless the target journal differs), no citations, no undefined abbreviations: context (1–2 sentences) → gap or aim → approach and data → main results with numbers ("Here we show…") → implication or limitation.
* IMRaD for papers: Introduction (context → gap → aim, ≤ 4 paragraphs), Methods (reproducible: reagents, versions, parameters, data and code availability; subsections), Results (what was found, with numbers and figure references; interpretation kept brief), Discussion (main finding → comparison with literature → limitations → implication; no new results).
* Tense: past for what was done and found in this study (Methods, Results); present for established knowledge, for what a figure or table shows ("Figure 2 shows…"), and for interpretation and conclusions drawn now.
* Figure legends/captions: first sentence states what is shown (no title-like fragments); then n, error-bar definition, tests, and abbreviations. Refer to figures by their content or file name in project files, not by assumed numbers.
* README and project notes: compact, key points, same rules; bullet lists allowed where listing parallel items; start with what the project does and the main result.
* Slides: text is short fragments, not full sentences; speaker notes and the references follow this skill.

## 6. Field vocabulary

Use the exact terms, spellings, and abbreviations that recent papers in the sub-field use; repeat a technical term instead of paraphrasing it; prefer official nomenclature (HGNC, HGVS, GO, SO, SI units); copy a form the target journal or lab already uses.
